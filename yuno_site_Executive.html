Introductory Section

Executive Summary for Leadership

Yuno faces an on-site inspection by the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) raising two high-materiality regulatory questions: (1) whether the payment orchestration model requires a Major Payment Institution (MPI) license under the Payment Services Act 2019 (PSA); and (2) whether Yuno has independent KYC/AML obligations with respect to the merchants it onboards.

Yuno's model has defensible arguments to sustain that its role is technological, not financial — it does not touch funds, does not settle, and does not assume credit risk. However, the regulation does not explicitly address orchestrators, and MAS may adopt an expansive interpretation.

There is also a critical risk not initially identified: licensed PSPs in Singapore operating through Yuno could face direct MAS pressure to suspend their relationship with Yuno under threat of sanctions against themselves — effectively halting Yuno's Singapore operations without any formal action against Yuno directly.

⚠ Overall Risk Assessment: HIGH An adverse determination in Singapore could trigger regulatory reviews in the Philippines, Thailand, and Indonesia. Immediate action is required on three fronts.

Section II — Core Issue

Does Yuno Require a License Under the PSA 2019?

The PSA establishes a modular licensing regime for seven categories of regulated payment services. MAS has flagged two specific categories for Yuno: (A) merchant acquisition services and (B) e-money issuance. Each is analyzed below, along with an additional risk not raised by MAS.

Section II · A

Merchant Acquisition Services — Gray Area

The PSA defines merchant acquisition services (First Schedule, Part 1, Activity D) as a service where the provider enters into a contract with a merchant to accept and process payment transactions, resulting in a transfer of money to the merchant, regardless of whether the service provider comes into possession of the money.

The MAS Guidelines PS-G01 (January 2025) clarify that this activity covers services where the provider "processes payment transactions from the merchant and processes payment receipts on behalf of the merchant," typically including POS terminals or online payment gateways.

Arguments Analysis

✓ Yuno's Arguments (does not apply)
  1. No acquisition contract. Yuno's agreement is a technology services agreement, not an acquiring contract. The actual acquiring contracts exist between each merchant and each downstream PSP.
  2. No settlement function. Funds flow exclusively PSP→Merchant. Yuno does not appear at any point in the monetary flow.
  3. No credit risk assumption. Chargeback risk and card network liability fall entirely on the PSPs.
  4. Technology provider exclusion. MAS FAQs (April 2024) explicitly exclude "technical service providers that support the provision of any payment service but do not enter into possession of money under that payment service."
  5. Industry analogy. Orchestrators like Spreedly and Gr4vy operate in common law jurisdictions as software providers, not acquirers.
✗ MAS Arguments (does apply)
  1. Non-possession clause. The definition expressly includes providers who do not "come into possession of the money" — arguably designed to capture intermediaries like orchestrators.
  2. Direct contract. Yuno does enter into a contract with the merchant and "provides payment services" in a broad sense — orchestration is indispensable for the payment to occur.
  3. Functional role. Yuno is the single integration point. From the merchant's perspective, Yuno is the entity that "enables" payments.
  4. Regional precedent. MAS cited an enforcement action against an orchestrator in the region, suggesting a more aggressive internal position on this model.
🔶 Assessment: HIGH-RISK GRAY AREA The technology provider exclusion in the 2024 FAQs is Yuno's strongest argument, but it does not carry the force of law — it is non-binding guidance. The "regardless of possession" clause is genuinely ambiguous. It is not advisable to rely solely on a favorable textual interpretation.
Section II · B

E-Money Issuance — Strong Position

The PSA defines e-money issuance as the issuance of electronically stored monetary value, denominated in or pegged to a currency, paid in advance, accepted by persons other than the issuer, and representing a claim against the issuer.

✓ Assessment: DOES NOT APPLY — Strong position Yuno does not issue e-money. No monetary value is stored in Yuno's systems. There is no prepaid balance. Merchants do not deposit funds with Yuno. The routing instruction transmitted by Yuno does not constitute issuance of stored value. This is Yuno's strongest argument and should be communicated clearly with supporting technical documentation at the inspection.
⚠ Residual future risk If Yuno were to implement prepaid features, cashback, or merchant wallets, this category would become immediately relevant. It is recommended to formally document that no current Yuno functionality involves value storage.
Section II · C — Unidentified Risk

Emerging Risk: Cross-Border Money Transfer

🚨 Critical Risk Not Identified by the GC

Amendments to the PSA effective April 4, 2024 expanded the definition of "cross-border money transfer service" to include those who, from Singapore, facilitate cross-border transfers between entities in different countries, even where money is not accepted or received in Singapore (Payment Services Amendment Act 2021, effective April 4, 2024).

Given that Yuno routes payment instructions for transactions involving merchants in Singapore with customers abroad, and operates from outside Singapore toward clients in Singapore, there is a plausible argument that Yuno "facilitates" cross-border transfers within the expanded definition. This risk warrants urgent and separate legal analysis.

Before the inspection (next 10 days)

Section III — Core Issue

Independent KYC/AML Obligations

The second issue raised by MAS is whether Yuno has independent KYC/KYB and AML/CFT obligations for the merchants it onboards, or whether it can rely entirely on the downstream PSPs' due diligence.

Section III · A

Regulatory Framework: MAS Notice PSN01

AML/CFT obligations for payment service providers in Singapore are set out in MAS Notice PSN01 on Prevention of Money Laundering and Countering the Financing of Terrorism – Specified Payment Services (latest version: April 2, 2024, amended effective July 1, 2025). PSN01 formally applies to entities licensed as MPI or SPI under the PSA.

⚡ Recent Enforcement Signal

In June 2025, MAS imposed composition penalties totaling SGD 960,000 on five licensed MPIs providing cross-border money transfer services, for breaches of PSN01. This was the first publicly reported instance of MAS enforcing penalties specifically on payment service providers under the PSA for AML/CFT breaches. If MAS imposes such sanctions on licensed entities, the scrutiny toward an unlicensed operator would be considerably higher. (Bird & Bird, "Payment service providers under the radar," June 27, 2025)

Section III · B

Three Structural Failures of Yuno's Current Model

Failure 1: The circular argument

If MAS determines that Yuno should hold a license, PSN01 compliance obligations follow by definition. Yuno cannot use the absence of a license as a shield against AML obligations when the very regulatory risk in dispute is whether a license should be held. MAS will reject this argument.

Failure 2: The unregulated facilitator risk

MAS has shown interest in closing gaps where unregulated facilitators may be used to circumvent controls. In March 2024, MAS proposed a notice for Organised Market Operators requiring AML/CFT for non-financial participants — a clear signal of the regulatory direction toward all links in the payments chain.

Failure 3: The agent risk for PSPs

Under Section 18(1) of the PSA, a licensee cannot provide payment services in Singapore through an unlicensed agent. If PSPs use Yuno as their merchant onboarding point, it could be argued that Yuno is acting as an unlicensed agent — a potential violation for both Yuno and the PSPs.

Section III · C

The Cross-Border Dimension

Yuno operates from Latin America without physical presence in Singapore. This creates two critical regulatory implications:

1. MAS jurisdictional reach

The PSA applies to entities that "carry on a business of providing a payment service in Singapore." MAS interprets this criterion functionally, not territorially. If Yuno actively solicits clients in Singapore, receives remuneration for services to Singapore merchants, and its platform is the integration point for Singapore consumer payments, MAS may conclude that Yuno "carries on business in Singapore." The absence of a local office is mitigating, but not determinative.

2. MAS's practical enforcement capacity

While direct enforcement against foreign entities is complex, MAS can: (a) block access to the Singapore market, (b) alert licensed local PSPs to suspend their relationship with Yuno under threat of sanctions against themselves, and (c) coordinate with regulators in Yuno's operating countries via memoranda of understanding. The most immediate and effective threat is (b).

Section III · D

Practical Recommendations — KYC/AML

Immediate implementations (before the inspection)

  • Independent sanctions screening. Implement screening of current and new merchants against: Consolidated UN Security Council Sanctions List, OFAC SDN List, MAS Designated Entities. Implementable within days via APIs from Refinitiv, Dow Jones Risk & Compliance, or similar. Low cost, very high regulatory signal value.
  • Enhanced KYB documentation. Raise the standard to include: (a) Ultimate Beneficial Owner (UBO) information up to 25% ownership threshold, (b) source of funds declaration for high-volume merchants, (c) verifiable business nature declarations.
  • Documented internal AML/CFT policy. Draft and implement a preliminary internal policy. MAS values evidence of a compliance culture even if implementation is in progress.
  • Basic transaction monitoring. Implement automated alerts for: unusually high transaction volumes, transactions in high-risk jurisdictions (FATF grey/black lists), patterns inconsistent with the merchant's profile.

Medium term

  • Formalize reliance on third parties. PSN01 permits reliance on third-party due diligence, but requires the principal to maintain ultimate responsibility and retain documentation. Formalize written agreements with PSPs clearly allocating AML/KYC responsibilities.
  • Local Singapore compliance representative. Designate an external local compliance officer as MAS's point of contact. Demonstrates willingness to submit to local supervision and may be decisive in shaping MAS's posture.
⚠ Risk Not Identified by the GC

Critical Risk: Section 18 PSA — The Agent Problem

🚨 Urgent Action Required

There is a material risk not mentioned in the initial communication that warrants priority attention: licensed PSPs in Singapore operating through Yuno to reach their merchants may be inadvertently in violation of Section 18(1) of the PSA, which prohibits licensees from providing payment services through unlicensed agents.

If MAS adopts this argument, the most immediate consequence would be that Stripe, Adyen, and local acquirers would face direct MAS pressure to suspend their integration with Yuno under threat of sanctions against themselves. This could halt Singapore operations far more quickly than any direct action against Yuno.

Immediate recommended actions

  • Urgently assess with each PSP's legal team whether the current contractual relationship exposes the PSPs to Section 18(1) risk. This is the greatest short-term operational threat.
  • Review whether PSP contracts define Yuno as an "agent" or as an independent technology provider. The contractual distinction is critical.
  • Coordinate with PSP legal teams to present a unified position to MAS reinforcing the purely technological nature of the relationship.
